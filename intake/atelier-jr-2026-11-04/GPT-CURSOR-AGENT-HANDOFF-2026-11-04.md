## 4. Storytelling standards

The owner repeatedly asks for **strong storytelling**. Grip the viewer in the opening five seconds and close with a meaningful payoff. Every sequence should contribute action, stakes, a question, new evidence, a reversal, emotional consequence, or resolution.

Practical implications:

- A hook should put a compelling situation, contradiction, danger, or question in motion. Avoid spending the opening explaining channel branding or general historical background.
- Establish who matters, what they want, what obstructs them, and what changes because of their choices.
- Use geography when it makes an action or constraint understandable. A map with no story purpose wastes attention.
- Make transitions causal. A new date or fact should change the situation rather than simply extend a list.
- A visual should show what the narration needs the viewer to understand or feel. Repeated portraits cannot carry every event.
- Build to an ending that answers the opening promise or leaves the exact historically warranted uncertainty. A loose string of caveats is not a payoff.
- Retention feedback may inform revisions, but do not invent analytics or treat platform speculation as established causation.

The calm archival voice can coexist with urgency, danger, and intense scenes. Non-graphic, historically supported violence and historical-thriller staging are permissible within the creative direction. Passive imagery should not replace the story's action merely because it is easier to generate.

## 7. Format and motion

Shorts are native **9:16**; long-form is native **16:9**. Previous Shorts work used 1080×1920 or larger. For the pilot, 1920×1080 delivery at 30 fps is a proposed technical fallback if the desk supplies no approved preset; it is not a recovered channel-wide standard. Confirm actual desk settings before production export.

Do not stretch portrait images or add blurred portrait fills to meet widescreen requirements. Recompose or generate proper widescreen masters. Preserve enough source resolution and overscan for planned crops without inventing detail through repeated enlargement.

Movement should have a dramatic function: reveal, approach, withdrawal, environmental danger, bodily action, or controlled attention. Vary scale and direction with intent. Protect faces, hand positions, geography, text, and evidence-sensitive objects.

Distinguish:

1. Deterministic editing/compositing: crops, camera movement over a painting, layer parallax, titles, routes, document masks, atmospheric overlays.
2. Generative video: physical movement, waves, sails, smoke, fabric, gestures, weather, action, or a camera move requiring newly synthesized scene detail.

Choose between them shot by shot. There is no approved fixed percentage of generative video, and a blanket slow push-in is not an adequate motion strategy. Conversely, making every second generative may waste credits and introduce avoidable continuity errors.

Inspect generative clips at the beginning, middle, end, and across the action. Reject texture drift, morphing faces, impossible hands, changing costume/architecture, invented text, rubbery motion, and glossy/glittering surface effects that damage the painted identity. Do not assume an acceptable first frame guarantees an acceptable shot.

The owner suggested visual changes approximately every 5–10 seconds, give or take. Treat this as pacing intent, not an obligation to generate a new painting on a timer. A 12-minute film could have 72–144 visual changes at that cadence, while using far fewer unique master paintings. Meaningful returns and details can support continuity; arbitrary repeated crops become filler.
