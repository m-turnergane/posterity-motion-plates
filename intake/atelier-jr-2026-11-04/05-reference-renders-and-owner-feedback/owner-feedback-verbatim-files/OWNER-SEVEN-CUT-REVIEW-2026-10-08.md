**Date:** 8 October 2026  
**Prepared for:** owner, Vellum, Pulse  
**Decision:** Dracula remains Day 17's standalone. Proceed with preparation; audition the proposed opening before locking a re-voice or more aggressive crop. The present review **amends** the earlier October 8 desk handoff; it does not create a new immutable opening law.

## Executive decision

The historical baseline was mischaracterized when the four initially selected winners were compared with After Troy. The additional owner-supplied catalog check puts *most of the remaining distributed standalones* around **8–10 seconds average watch**. After Troy's approximately **9–10 seconds is therefore ordinary, not demonstrably a format-wide regression**. Hampi, Cahokia, Hattusa, Persepolis and Ani are stronger exceptions worth investigating. The very early 3–5-view uploads should not be used as meaningful retention comparisons.

The published visuals support one specific editorial repair: **Chapter II makes a promise about a daughter sacrificed for wind, then holds a nearly unchanging, generic series-prelude hall from ~3.5 to ~15.5 seconds** before identifying Agamemnon and the fleet. Chapter IV's frieze is shorter (~6 to ~11.5 seconds) and supplies causally relevant information about Agamemnon's household. Do not treat these as equivalent administrative interruptions. Nor should the desk ban long holds: Hattusa holds a map from ~6 to ~21 seconds while its narration continues to teach geography, the empire's identity and the city's material character.

**Important confound:** the apparent winners and After Troy IV have drastically different audience composition. After Troy IV's viewers were 76% aged 18–34 and 32.4% Canadian; Cahokia's were 15% aged 18–34 and 87.1% Canadian. The evidence supports a *creative hypothesis* about storytelling continuity, not a causal verdict that editing alone explains the watch-time differences. Audience *matching* remains unresolved even when For You provides exposure.

### What improves across the stronger examples

A useful shared *editorial* feature is not “the camera must move” or “name the subject in the first second.” It is **promise → meaningful proof or a changed understanding → new consequence**. Cahokia pays its contrast by ~4s, Persepolis announces the burn by ~4s, Ani states the reversal by ~7s, Hattusa gives an imperial-status surprise then loss by ~5s, and Hampi identifies the threatened capital by ~4s. Their subsequent descriptions generally move through place, time, identity and consequences without reintroducing the format. This is a qualitative pattern from selected clips, not a statistically validated cause of retention.

**Chapter II's specific failure mode:** its hook creates *referent debt* (whose daughter?) and *causal debt* (why a bride for wind?), then spends ~12s on broad series language before moving toward answers. The scene is aesthetically coherent, but the viewer cannot efficiently connect the promise with the impending event. This is a sharper and more useful criticism than “static pictures are bad.”

**Chapter IV is different:** its 6–11.5s passage about Agamemnon's house is specific plot context, not only series administration. If the passage were removed, the crime chain could become harder to follow. Edit for continuity and necessity, not for a blanket ban on context.

### Where the statistical case stops

- **Audience composition differs too much for simple causal comparisons.** Supplied screenshots: after Troy IV (18–34 **76%**, 55+ **3%**, Canada **32.4%**); Hampi (**26%**, **31%**, **71.4%**); Cahokia (**15%**, **40%**, **87.1%**); Persepolis (**36%**, **28%**, **56.7%**); Ani (**20%**, **40%**, **65.3%**). Geography and age may reflect topic, recommendation timing or platform matching, not necessarily viewing preference.
- **Three-second retention is not a reliable ranking by itself.** Cahokia **52%**, Hampi **43%**, Ani **40%**, After Troy IV **40%**, Persepolis **30%**; their average-watch ordering differs. Persepolis is **14%** at ten seconds versus Chapter IV **13%**, yet their reported average watches are **15.16s** and **10.04s**.
- **The TikTok metrics cannot automatically be integrated into one single-pass survival curve.** Under the explicit assumptions that all percentages and average watch refer to the *same play cohort*, retention never increases, and each play contributes no more than the video's 72.63-second length, Persepolis's 30% at 3s and 14% at 10s would imply a **maximum possible mean of ~13.87s** (3 + 7×0.30 + (72.63−10)×0.14). Reported average watch is **15.16s**. This shows the naive assumptions do not hold for those published dashboard figures; possible explanations include loops/replays, different metric populations, incomplete or asynchronous refresh, or a non-survival interpretation of the curve. It does **not** prove one particular explanation. Do not assert that this case proves superior after-10s story retention.
- **Average watch is not the only audience outcome.** After Troy IV has 24 likes and 4 saves on 507 views (roughly 4.7% likes/view and 0.79% saves/view), versus Hampi 12 likes and 0 saves on 228, Cahokia 37/3 on 652, Persepolis 5/1 on 219 and Ani 7/1 on 196. Counts and per-view rates are too small and audience-mixed to claim higher *quality* or genuine unique-person propensity, but they caution against describing the serial as a wholesale engagement failure.
- **Metric refresh and scope vary.** Examples in separate screenshot tabs report total viewers larger than overview views (e.g., Ani's 214 versus 196). These cannot be combined as same-grain, same-snapshot numbers without verifying how the pages update.
- **The first seven 3–5-view uploads belong to a limited-distribution measurement regime**, distinct from later manually published uploads. The publishing-method switch preceded improved reach; it does not prove TikTok identified or penalized agent posting.
- **The other older distributed standalones cluster near 8–10s** per owner check; Sigiriya is lower (6.61s). We have their reported range rather than a complete raw row set, so do not publish an invented precise mean or sample variance.

## New, narrow editorial standard for Vellum

**“Pay the hook's debt.”** An opening creates a question, mystery or reversal. Its next passage should (a) clarify a referent, (b) deliver evidence, (c) show an immediate consequence, or (d) supply necessary spatial/historical orientation. A calm shot or atlas can earn a sustained hold by doing that work. A generic series announcement that postpones the answers is a candidate for removal. The editor should make this judgment on the *rendered* opening, with captions viewed on a phone-sized frame.

Use a flexible sequence, not a rigid stopwatch: **promise → partial payoff / orientation → escalation**. Keep truthful qualification, tonal restraint and a purposeful Chronicle Reveal. Do not impose universal proper-name, first-second action, ban-on-context, cuts-per-minute or two-second title-disappearance rules.

## Day 17: Dracula production decision

1. **Proceed with the episode.** Dracula remains the approved October standalone; do not delay unrelated research or plate planning.
2. **Keep both VO candidates available pending a genuine sound-on editorial comparison.** Current approved line: “Before Dracula was a vampire's name, it belonged to a prince at war with the sultan.” Concise alternative from the original desk: “Dracula was a prince's name. That prince fought the Ottoman sultan.” Both require historical/continuity approval; this visual-frame audit cannot certify intonation or diction. Re-voice only if a tighter line actually improves the promise-to-conflict progression in the produced opening.
3. **Open on a readable historical relationship.** The rider versus the opposing camp can work. Ensure the camp, great pavilion, and rider's facing read on a phone. Keep the no-face/historical locks and do not equate a mid-step painted horse with motion. Do not automatically promote the raid to frame one or heighten uncertain assassination intent.
4. **Avoid the Chapter II mistake.** After the vampire-name contrast and prince/sultan conflict, move into an approved historical detail, decision or immediate consequence. No generic “the true story behind Dracula” restart and no lengthy aesthetic holding chamber that delays specificity.
5. **Keep the title and story aligned.** “BEFORE THE VAMPIRE” can carry the comparison silently; judge the actual visual overlap, caption readability and animation rhythm rather than a fixed two-second departure.
6. **Defer the new mid-video follow prompt** until the episode demonstrates a coherent opening/body. Do not add another interruptive element while changing the opening package.

## Salem and next-cycle learning

- Trial the alternating chapters and standalones for **editorial variety and workable production cadence**, not as an algorithmically proven 48-hour rest period.
- Each chapter should contain a standalone promise, orientation and local payoff. A small visual chapter marker or native Reel linking is preferable to an extended spoken series admission. Necessary backstory may be included where it directly advances today's event.
- For the next releases capture views and retention at **0:03 and 0:10**, plus a fuller curve where available; average watch and completion; directly attributed followers/saves/shares; and **audience age/country mix**. Snapshot at comparable upload ages, and keep platform definitions separate.
- Compare within reasonable topic/audience slices where possible; do not declare a Halloween or Salem opening successful simply because it outperforms an architectural-capital upload on raw average watch.
- Use creative reviews as **diagnostic evidence**. If several independent examples recur, then promote the principle into an editorial default. No single historical upload authorizes a permanent formula.
