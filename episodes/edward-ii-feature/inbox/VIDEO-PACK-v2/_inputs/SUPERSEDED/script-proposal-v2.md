## Movement 1. The sealed letter
**Time:** about 0:00 to 0:40 · **Music:** Act I. Mystery

**Purpose:** Poses the contradiction and the central question within the opening seconds: a royal brother writing to a buried king.

**VO**

01. The king's own brother sealed a letter for a king that England had already buried. `[C09, C10]`
02. The brother was Edmund, Earl of Kent. The buried king was his half-brother, Edward the Second. `[NR-1]`
03. England had announced Edward's death, and Kent had been there when Edward was laid to rest at Gloucester. `[C10, C20]`
04. Just over two years after that funeral, Kent was condemned for plotting to set his brother free, and he was beheaded at Winchester. `[C06, C28]`
05. So why would a king's brother risk his life to rescue a man the whole kingdom had mourned? `[question]`
06. And how much of this story can the evidence actually prove? `[question]`

**Evidence reveal:** Kent's sealed letter (DOC-K1) seen, never legible. The funeral at Gloucester. Winchester.

**Causal bridge:** If the king was dead and buried, why was his brother writing to him? To answer, we have to go back to how the two brothers ended up on opposite sides.

## Movement 2. Brothers
**Time:** about 0:40 to 2:10 · **Music:** Act I. Mystery

**Purpose:** Establishes the human center and the early contradiction: Kent helped bring Edward down.

**VO**

07. Edmund of Kent was a younger son of King Edward the First, by the old king's second marriage. `[NR-1]`
08. Edward the Second was his much older half-brother, and for years Kent served him as one of the great lords of the realm. `[NR-1, NR-2]`
09. But Edward's reign turned bitter. His favourites had turned England's barons against him. `[NR-3 (Short CS-v3)]`
10. And in the end his own wife, Queen Isabella, turned against him too, alongside her ally, a lord of the Welsh borderlands named Roger Mortimer. `[NR-3 (Short CS-v3)]`
11. In the autumn, Isabella and Roger Mortimer landed in England with an invading force. The Earl of Kent landed with them. `[C11]`
12. The records tell us what Kent did. They do not tell us why he did it. `[C11]`
13. Edward fled west to Chepstow, on the border with Wales, and sailed from there. Soon afterwards he was captured in South Wales. `[C25, C26]`
14. He was taken to Kenilworth Castle, in the middle of England, and there he lost his crown. `[C25]`
15. His teenage son, also named Edward, was crowned Edward the Third. Kent's own nephew was now king. `[NR-4]`
16. But the real power belonged to the boy's mother, Isabella, and to Roger Mortimer. `[NR-4 (Short CS-v3)]`
17. So the Earl of Kent had helped to bring down his own brother. Everything he did later runs against that fact. `[C11 (actions only)]`

**Evidence reveal:** None (context movement). Map: Chepstow, South Wales, Kenilworth.

**Causal bridge:** Kent's side has won and Edward is a prisoner. The next question is what happened to the prisoner.

## Movement 3. A death announced
**Time:** about 2:10 to 3:30 · **Music:** Act I. Mystery

**Purpose:** Lays out the official story and its thin points, with each one attributed.

**VO**

18. In the spring, the deposed king was moved again, to Berkeley Castle, in Gloucestershire. `[C25]`
19. In late September, word went out that Edward of Caernarfon, the former king, was dead. `[C20]`
20. The news reached the young king at Lincoln a couple of days later. `[C20]`
21. No surviving official record says how he had died. `[NR-5]`
22. A chronicler of the time, Adam Murimuth, says that local notables were called to see the body: abbots, priors, knights, and leading townsmen of Bristol and Gloucester. `[C04]`
23. But Murimuth adds a strange detail. He says they looked at it only superficially. `[C04]`
24. Historians think that at the funeral itself, a wooden effigy of the king was probably displayed. `[C04]`
25. Edward was buried at Gloucester just before Christmas, about three months after his death was announced. `[C06, C20]`
26. The Earl of Kent was there. No record says whether he ever saw his brother's body. `[C10]`
27. England moved on under its new rulers. `[C06 (interval)]`
28. Then reports began to reach Kent that his brother was still alive. `[C09]`

**Evidence reveal:** Murimuth's 'superficial' viewing (attributed). The effigy (historians, probably). Kent at the funeral, never shown seeing the body.

**Causal bridge:** The official story is in place, and Kent was at the funeral. So what could make him doubt it? Reports from far away.

## Movement 4. The rescue
**Time:** about 3:30 to 5:45 · **Music:** Act II. Danger

**Purpose:** Belief becomes a fatal act. Kent's message (DOC-K1) is introduced and kept separate from Fieschi's letter.

**VO**

29. According to an English chronicle called the Brut, Kent went to see the pope at Avignon, where the pope held his court, and spoke of a common report in England that Edward was alive. `[C09, C14b (background)]`
30. In his later confession, Kent said the pope himself charged him, on his blessing, to work for his brother's release. `[C09]`
31. According to Kent's own confession, a friar had assured him that Edward was alive, claiming that the devil had told him so. `[C09; friar's claim as reported in Kent's confession, no endorsement, no on-screen devil visual]`
32. And the Brut says that friars told him where. Edward, they said, was being held in Corfe Castle, in Dorset. `[C09]`
33. Kent was not acting alone. His confession names powerful backers, among them an archbishop, a bishop and several knights. `[C08]`
34. The archbishop was William Melton of York. The confession says he promised Kent five thousand pounds. `[C02c]`
35. One modern historian, Kathryn Warner, has traced around seventy men who supported him. `[C07]`
36. Then, as the Brut tells it, Kent came to Corfe himself, with a letter for his brother, closed and sealed with his own seal. `[C09]`
37. At Corfe he gave it to a man named John Deveril. `[C09]`
38. The Brut says the letter never reached Edward. Deveril took it to Roger Mortimer instead. `[C09]`
39. One chronicle claims to quote its opening words. The letter itself has never been found. `[C05c]`
40. At the parliament held at Winchester, Kent's confession was put before the lords. `[C05f, C28]`
41. A copy of that confession still survives, written into a chronicle manuscript about seventy years later. `[C05a]`
42. The official record of his trial is lost. `[C05d]`
43. The Earl of Kent was condemned for plotting to free a man the government said was dead, and in March he was beheaded at Winchester. `[C28]`
44. Adam Murimuth called the whole story false. But he also thought Kent's death was unjust. `[C18]`
45. Kent left behind a widow, Margaret, and their children. `[C13]`

**Evidence reveal:** DOC-K1: sealed, never found, opening only claimed by the Brut. The confession copy (around 1400). The lost trial record.

**Causal bridge:** Kent is dead, and his plot rested on a story one chronicler called false. But the men who condemned him were about to fall.

## Movement 5. The fall of Mortimer
**Time:** about 5:45 to 7:10 · **Music:** Act II. Danger

**Purpose:** The regime's own record contradicts itself: Edward murdered, Kent deceived, and Lord Berkeley's denial.

**VO**

46. That autumn, the young king struck back. At Nottingham Castle, Edward the Third's party seized Roger Mortimer. `[C27]`
47. In November, parliament met, and Mortimer was charged. `[C12]`
48. The charges said Mortimer had Edward sent to Berkeley, and had him murdered there. `[C12]`
49. The same parliament turned to the Earl of Kent. It said Mortimer's circle had tricked Kent into believing that his brother was alive. `[C05e, C12]`
50. And it said Kent had been fooled about a man who had been dead for a long time. `[C12]`
51. Mortimer was hanged at Tyburn, outside London, at the end of November. `[C12]`
52. Within weeks of Mortimer's fall, Kent's widow asked parliament to reopen his case. `[C13]`
53. So the new government now had a clear story. The old king had been murdered, and Kent had been the victim of a cruel trick. `[C12, C05e (summary of the roll)]`
54. But one statement in the same record does not fit so neatly. `[C01a, C01b]`
55. Thomas, Lord Berkeley, held the castle where Edward was said to have died, and he had to answer for the death. `[C01a]`
56. The parliament roll records Lord Berkeley saying he never consented to the death, and never knew of it until that parliament. `[C01a]`
57. He also said that at the time he had been ill, away from the castle. `[C01a]`
58. Was Berkeley denying a murder, or denying that the king had died at all? Historians disagree about what he meant. `[C01b]`

**Evidence reveal:** The November parliament roll (C12, C05e). Lord Berkeley's words in the roll (C01a). The widow's petition (C13).

**Causal bridge:** The government's story is official now, but the man who held the castle says he never knew. Years later, a different document tells a different story.

## Movement 6. A different letter
**Time:** about 7:10 to 9:10 · **Music:** Act III. Investigation

**Purpose:** A second, separate document reopens the question. Different writer, recipient and decade; the only touchpoint is the letter's own claim about Kent.

**VO**

59. Years later, a very different document was written. `[C14]`
60. A notary of the pope, Manuel Fieschi, wrote to the young king. `[C14b]`
61. Fieschi was an Italian churchman who would later become bishop of Vercelli, in northern Italy. `[NR-6]`
62. His letter has no date, and it does not say where it was written. Historians think it was written roughly a decade after the funeral. `[C14, C14b]`
63. It survives only as a copy, made decades later, in a register of the bishops of Maguelone, near Montpellier, in the south of France. `[C14, C03d]`
64. This is not Kent's letter. It has a different writer, a different reader, and it was written years after Kent's death. Nothing in the record connects the two. `[C17]`
65. Fieschi tells Edward the Third that he is setting down, in his own hand, what he heard in his father's confession. `[C03a]`
66. The letter claims that the former king escaped from Berkeley Castle in a servant's clothes. `[C03a]`
67. It claims he was hidden at Corfe Castle for a year and a half. `[C03a]`
68. Then, it says, he heard that the Earl of Kent had been beheaded for saying that he was alive. `[C03a, C17]`
69. That is the only place where Fieschi's letter touches Kent's story, and it is the letter's own claim. `[C17]`
70. From there, the letter says, he crossed to Ireland for some months, then came back to England dressed as a hermit, and crossed the sea to the Low Countries. `[C03a, C03b]`
71. It says he travelled through Normandy and the south of France to Avignon, where Pope John kept him for more than fifteen days. `[C03a]`
72. Then on, it says, through Paris, the duchy of Brabant and the city of Cologne, and across Germany to Milan. `[C03a]`
73. And it leaves him living as a hermit in Lombardy, in remote hermitages near Milan and in the diocese of Pavia. `[C03a, C03c]`
74. That is what the letter claims. Whether any of it happened is a separate question. `[C03a (framing)]`

**Evidence reveal:** DOC-F1: undated, no place of writing, surviving only as a later copy in the Maguelone cartulary. Itinerary map: Chepstow to Milan only with certainty.

**Causal bridge:** The letter makes a full claim. Now the viewer needs the tools to weigh it against everything else.

_Movement words: 307_

## Movement 7. What the evidence can bear
**Time:** about 9:10 to 11:15 · **Music:** Act III. Investigation

**Purpose:** Separates what is established from what is only attributed, gives the majority view fairly, and states what stays open.

**VO**

75. Here is the hard part. A letter can be genuinely old, and genuinely written by the man who signed it, and still tell a story that is not true. `[INF-1 (ours), C16d]`
76. Most historians hold that Edward the Second died at Berkeley. `[C16e]`
77. The medieval historian David Carpenter argues that Edward almost certainly died there. `[C16a]`
78. On the other side, the historian Ian Mortimer, a modern writer not to be confused with Roger, argues that the death was faked, and that Edward was still alive when Kent made his move. `[C16b]`
79. So what is the strongest case that Edward survived? `[framing]`
80. First, Kent's own belief. He was the king's brother, he had been at the funeral, and he still staked his life on the reports. `[C10, C09, C28]`
81. Second, he was not alone. Kathryn Warner argues that his supporters were many, and often close to Edward. `[C16c]`
82. Third, a letter from the archbishop of York, which still survives, reported news that the former king was alive. Its modern editor dates it to about two months before Kent's death. `[C02a, C02b]`
83. And fourth, Lord Berkeley's words in parliament, which can be read as a denial that the king had died at all. `[C01a, C01b, C16b]`
84. Now the strongest reply. `[framing]`
85. Kent could have been deceived. The new government's own record says he was, and the modern editor of the archbishop's letter thinks the archbishop was misled. `[C05e, C02d]`
86. There was a body. Local notables were called to view it, even if one chronicler says they looked only superficially. `[C04]`
87. There was a royal funeral, with Kent himself in attendance. `[C10]`
88. And Fieschi's letter is undated, written years later, and survives only as a copy. Older historians found it remarkable, but could not believe it. `[C14, C16d]`
89. So what can the evidence establish? `[framing]`
90. It can establish that no surviving official record of the time said how he died, and that the new government later called it murder. `[C20, NR-5, C12]`
91. It can establish that Kent acted as though his brother lived, and that he was executed for it. `[C09, C28, C16a]`
92. What it cannot establish is where Edward the Second really was on the day his brother died. `[C16a, C16b, C16e (open question)]`

**Evidence reveal:** Melton's surviving letter (paraphrase only). The parliament roll again. The body and the funeral. The cartulary copy.

**Causal bridge:** The evidence cannot place Edward on the day Kent died. That leaves the one thing we know for certain: what Kent did.

## Movement 8. Return to Kent

**Purpose:** Emotional resolution on the human center; honest close on the open question; narrated tagline.

**VO**

93. Edmund, Earl of Kent, helped take his brother's crown. `[C11]`
94. He stood at his brother's funeral. `[C10]`
95. Then, just over two years later, he gave his life trying to set that same brother free. `[C06, C28]`

98. History remembers the victors. We remember the rest. `[tagline]`

**Evidence reveal:** None new. Callback to the sealed letter.
