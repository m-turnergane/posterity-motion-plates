L08. Edward the Second was his much older half-brother, and for years Kent served him as a great lord.
L09. But Edward's reign turned bitter. His favourites had turned England's barons against him.
L10. In the end his own queen, Isabella, turned against him too, with her ally Roger Mortimer, a lord of the Welsh borderlands.
L11. In the autumn, Isabella and Roger Mortimer landed in England with an invading force. The Earl of Kent landed with them.
L12. The records tell us what Kent did. They do not tell us why he did it.
L13. Edward fled west to Chepstow, on the border with Wales, and sailed from there. Soon afterwards he was captured in South Wales.
L14. He was taken to Kenilworth Castle, in the middle of England, and there he lost his crown.
L15. His teenage son, also named Edward, was crowned Edward the Third. Kent's own nephew was now king.
L16. But the real power belonged to the boy's mother, Isabella, and to Roger Mortimer.
L17. So the Earl of Kent had helped to bring down his own brother. Everything he did later runs against that fact.
L18. In the spring, the deposed king was moved again, to Berkeley Castle, in Gloucestershire.
L19. In late September, word went out that Edward of Caernarfon, the former king, was dead.
L20. The news reached the young king at Lincoln a couple of days later.
L21. No surviving official record says how he had died.
L22. A chronicler of the time, Adam Murimuth, says that local notables were called to see the body: abbots, priors, knights, and leading townsmen of Bristol and Gloucester.
L23. But Murimuth adds a strange detail. He says they looked at it only superficially.
L24. Historians think that at the funeral itself, a wooden effigy of the king was probably displayed.
L25. Edward was buried at Gloucester just before Christmas, about three months after his death was announced.
L26. The Earl of Kent was there. No record says whether he ever saw his brother's body.
L27. England moved on under its new rulers.
L28. Then reports began to reach Kent that his brother was still alive.
L29. According to an English chronicle called the Brut, Kent went to see the pope at Avignon, where the pope held his court, and spoke of a common report in England that Edward was alive.
L30. In his later confession, Kent said the pope himself charged him, on his blessing, to work for his brother's release.
L31. According to Kent's own confession, a friar had assured him that Edward was alive, claiming that the devil had told him so.
L32. And the Brut says that friars told him where. Edward, they said, was being held in Corfe Castle, in Dorset.
L33. Kent was not acting alone. His confession names powerful backers, among them an archbishop, a bishop and several knights.
L34. The archbishop was William Melton of York. The confession says he promised Kent five thousand pounds.
L35. One modern historian, Kathryn Warner, has traced around seventy men who supported him.
L36. Then, as the Brut tells it, Kent came to Corfe himself, with a letter for his brother, closed and sealed with his own seal.
L37. At Corfe he gave it to a man named John Deveril.
L38. The Brut says the letter never reached Edward. Deveril took it to Roger Mortimer instead.
L39. One chronicle claims to quote its opening words. The letter itself has never been found.
L40. At the parliament held at Winchester, Kent's confession was put before the lords.
L41. A copy of that confession still survives, written into a chronicle manuscript about seventy years later.
L42. The official record of his trial is lost.
L43. The Earl of Kent was condemned for plotting to free a man the government said was dead, and in March he was beheaded at Winchester.
L44. Adam Murimuth called the whole story false. But he also thought Kent's death was unjust.
L45. Kent left behind a widow, Margaret, and their children.
L46. That autumn, the young king struck back. At Nottingham Castle, Edward the Third's party seized Roger Mortimer.
L47. In November, parliament met, and Mortimer was charged.
L48. The charges said Mortimer had Edward sent to Berkeley, and had him murdered there.
L49. The same parliament turned to the Earl of Kent. It said Mortimer's circle had tricked Kent into believing that his brother was alive.
L50. And it said Kent had been fooled about a man who had been dead for a long time.
L51. Mortimer was hanged at Tyburn, outside London, at the end of November.
L52. Within weeks of Mortimer's fall, Kent's widow asked parliament to reopen his case.
L53. So the new government now had a clear story. The old king had been murdered, and Kent had been the victim of a cruel trick.
L54. But one statement in the same record has been read in two ways.
L55. Thomas, Lord Berkeley, held the castle where Edward was said to have died, and he had to answer for the death.
L56. The parliament roll records Lord Berkeley saying he never consented to the death, and never knew of it until that parliament.
L57. He also said that at the time he had been ill, away from the castle.
L57a. The same parliament condemned two of Edward's keepers, Thomas Gurney and William Ockley, for his murder.
L57b. Early the next year, a jury of knights cleared Lord Berkeley of the king's death. Yet the same record says the king was murdered by keepers Berkeley had appointed.
L58. So the record itself treats the death as a murder. Some historians still read Berkeley's first words as a denial that the king had died at all. Others read them as a lord denying any part in a killing.
L59. Years later, a very different letter was written.
L60. A notary of the pope, an Italian churchman named Manuel Fieschi, wrote to the young king.
L62. The letter has no date. Historians think it was written roughly a decade after the funeral.
L63. It survives only as a later copy, in a register from Maguelone, in the south of France.
L64. This is not Kent's letter. It has a different writer and a different reader, and nothing in the record connects the two.
L65. Fieschi tells Edward the Third that he is setting down, in his own hand, what he heard in his father's confession.
L66. The letter claims that the former king escaped from Berkeley Castle in a servant's clothes.
L67. It claims he was hidden at Corfe Castle for a year and a half.
L68. Then, it says, he heard that the Earl of Kent had been beheaded for saying that he was alive.
L69. That is the only place where Fieschi's letter touches Kent's story, and it is the letter's own claim.
L70. From there, the letter says, he kept moving: to Ireland for some months, then back to England dressed as a hermit, and across the sea into Europe.
L71. It says he reached Avignon, where Pope John kept him for more than fifteen days.
L73. And it leaves him living as a hermit in Lombardy, in northern Italy.
L74. That is what the letter claims. Whether any of it happened is a separate question.
L75. Here is the hard part. A letter can be genuinely old, and genuinely written by the man who signed it, and still tell a story that is not true.
L76. Most historians hold that Edward the Second died at Berkeley.
L77. The medieval historian David Carpenter argues that Edward almost certainly died there.
L78. On the other side, the historian Ian Mortimer, a modern writer not to be confused with Roger, argues that the death was faked, and that Edward was still alive when Kent made his move.
L79. So what is the strongest case that Edward survived?
L80. First, Kent's own belief. He was the king's brother, he had been at the funeral, and he still staked his life on the reports.
L81. Second, he was not alone. Kathryn Warner argues that his supporters were many, and often close to Edward.
L82. Third, a letter from the archbishop of York, which still survives, reported news that the former king was alive. Its modern editor dates it to about two months before Kent's death.
L83. And fourth, Lord Berkeley's words in parliament, which can be read as a denial that the king had died at all.
L84. Now the strongest reply.
L85. Kent could have been deceived. The new government's own record says he was, and the modern editor of the archbishop's letter thinks the archbishop was misled.
L86. There was a body. Local notables were called to view it, even if one chronicler says they looked only superficially.
L86a. And the same record that holds Berkeley's words also condemns Edward's keepers for his murder.
L87. There was a royal funeral, with Kent himself in attendance.
L88. And Fieschi's letter is undated, written years later, and survives only as a copy. Older historians found it remarkable, but could not believe it.
L89. So what can the evidence establish?
L90. It can establish that no surviving official record of the time said how he died, and that the new government later called it murder.
L91. It can establish what Kent did, and that he and others believed the reports that his brother was alive.
L92. But belief is not proof. None of it shows that Edward survived, and most historians still hold that he died at Berkeley.
L93. Edmund, Earl of Kent, helped take his brother's crown.
L94. He stood at his brother's funeral.
L95. Then, just over two years later, he gave his life trying to set that same brother free.
L96. Kent made his choice, and he paid for it with his head.
L97. As the Brut tells it, the letter he sealed for his brother never reached him. It went to Roger Mortimer instead.
L98. History remembers the victors. We remember the rest.
