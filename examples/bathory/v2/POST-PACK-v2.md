**Description**
```
Her servants were executed. Elizabeth Báthory was never tried.

Elizabeth Báthory, a countess of one of Hungary's greatest noble families, is remembered as the Blood Countess. For months, complaints had spread that young women in her household were disappearing. King Matthias of Hungary ordered the Palatine, György Thurzó, the highest-ranking official in the kingdom, to investigate.

But Thurzó was no stranger. On 3 January 1604, the day before he died, her husband Ferenc Nádasdy wrote to his friend Thurzó asking him to protect his wife and family. On 29 December 1610, Thurzó's men entered her manor house in the village of Csejte (today Čachtice, Slovakia) as she sat down to supper. In a letter to his wife the next day, Thurzó wrote that she was being taken up into the castle.

Four of her servants were taken to Bytča, where they were questioned under torture on 2 January 1611. A judgment was issued on 7 January, and three of them were executed at once. The fourth was imprisoned. Báthory herself was never heard on the charges. She was confined in Csejte castle, and although King Matthias repeatedly pressed for a trial, Thurzó prevented one. She died in confinement on 21 August 1614.

Why was she never tried? Some historians, such as Irma Szádeczky-Kardoss, argue the case was built to ruin her. Others read her family's arrangements as a sign that they believed she was involved and wanted to protect the family's property and honour. Over three hundred witnesses were questioned in the inquests, and many admitted that what they reported was hearsay. No court ever decided.
```
